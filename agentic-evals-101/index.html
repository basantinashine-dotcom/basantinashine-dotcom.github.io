Agentic Evals 101

Agents don’t just answer. They plan, call tools, change things and adapt. That makes them powerful, and much harder to test. This page walks through how to evaluate them, one idea at a time.

What is an agent eval?

A test for a whole process, not one reply

An eval gives an AI system a task, then applies grading logic to what it did. For a chatbot, that means grading one answer. For an agent, it means grading a chain of decisions, tool calls and the final state of the world.

Sources: Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents ↗ · OpenAI, Agent evals and trace grading ↗

Why agents break

Small errors compound

If each step works 95% of the time, a 10-step task succeeds only about 60% of the time, and a 20-step task about 36%. Reliability per step matters more than you’d think.

Reliability

Lucky once vs. right every time

pass@k asks: did the agent succeed at least once in k tries? pass^k asks: did it succeed every time? Users experience the second. In tau-bench, even strong agents succeeded on under half the tasks, and far fewer succeeded every time.

Source: Yao et al., tau-bench (Sierra; ICLR 2025) ↗

Outcome vs. path

Grade what happened, not the exact route

Agents often find valid solutions you didn’t anticipate. Grade the end result, and only check the path where rules truly matter, such as verifying identity before a refund.

Sources: Anthropic ↗ · OpenAI, trace grading ↗ · Google Cloud, Evaluate Gen AI agents ↗

Three kinds of graders

Code, models and humans

Code checks are fast and exact but rigid. LLM judges scale to subjective quality but carry biases. Humans are the most accurate and the slowest. Strong eval systems layer all three.

Source: Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents ↗

The judge has blind spots

Trust, but verify your LLM judge

LLM judges can favor the first answer they see, longer answers, or answers that sound like themselves. Always measure how often your judge agrees with expert humans before you rely on it.

Source: Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (NeurIPS 2023) ↗

Where good evals come from

Start with real failures

The best eval suites aren’t invented in a meeting. They come from reading real traces, labeling what went wrong, grouping failures, and turning the most important ones into tests. Then fix, rerun and repeat.

Source: Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar, AI Evals FAQ ↗

Beyond accuracy

Cost, consistency and honest benchmarks

A slightly more accurate agent that costs 10 times more may not be better. Benchmarks need holdout sets, or agents learn shortcuts. Track accuracy, cost, latency and consistency together.

Sources: Kapoor et al., AI Agents That Matter ↗ · OpenAI, SWE-bench Verified ↗ · METR, long tasks ↗

From my work

How I validated a critic LLM at scale

At Amazon, my team generated millions of search ad variants from keywords and product details, far more than people could ever review. So every ad had to pass rule checks and a critic LLM before it was stored, and that critic agreed with human-labeled examples 99% of the time.

How we got there

  1. Ground both sidesThe writer and the critic were given the same product details, so the critic could check each claim against the source.
  2. Layer the checksRules caught what code can check, like headline and description length and too much overlap between them. The critic scored the rest, each score with its own pass mark.
  3. Close the loopAn ad that failed went back to be rewritten. Only ads that passed every check were stored and served.
  4. Check against peoplePeople labeled a sample of the ads, using a defined sampling plan and annotation tools. The critic agreed with their labels 99% of the time.
  5. Tune in configurationThe critic’s model, prompts and pass marks lived in configuration, so scientists could adjust them without changing code. Changes were measured on offline benchmark datasets, and new markets and use cases launched through online experiments.
Interview prep deck

Practice: evals questions for AI PM interviews

Ten questions interviewers ask about evals. Tap a card to see what a strong answer covers.

  1. How would you evaluate a customer-support agent that can issue refunds?

    • Define success as the right end state: correct refund amount, correct account, policy followed.
    • Grade the outcome by checking the database state, plus hard rule checks on the path (identity verified before any refund).
    • Build test cases from real tickets, including edge cases and users trying to bend the policy.
    • Run each task several times and report consistency. A refund agent that’s right 80% of the time isn’t shippable.
    • Weight errors by cost: a wrong refund matters more than an awkward reply.

  2. Your LLM judge says quality went up, but users complain more. What do you do?

    • Trust the users first. The judge is a proxy, and it has drifted from what users value.
    • Read the complaint traces and label what’s actually going wrong.
    • Check the judge against human labels on those cases (agreement, false positives).
    • Look for judge biases, like preferring longer or more confident answers.
    • Fix the rubric, recalibrate on fresh human labels, and add the new failures to the test set.

  3. How do you build a golden dataset, and how do you keep it from going stale?

    • Start from real traffic and real failures, not made-up examples.
    • Have domain experts label it with a written rubric, and measure how often labelers agree.
    • Cover common cases, edge cases and high-risk cases on purpose.
    • Refresh it on a schedule and whenever production behavior shifts.
    • Keep a holdout slice no one tunes against.

  4. When would you use code-based grading instead of an LLM judge?

    • Use code whenever the answer is checkable: exact values, schema, tool called with the right arguments, tests passing, final state.
    • Code is fast, cheap, repeatable and has no bias.
    • Use an LLM judge for subjective qualities like tone, helpfulness and faithfulness to sources.
    • Best practice is layering: cheap code checks first, judges on what’s left, humans to calibrate.

  5. The agent scores 85% but fails on the same customer every third time. Is it ready to ship?

    • Not yet. An average hides inconsistency, and users feel the bad runs.
    • Measure pass^k: does it succeed on every one of k repeated tries?
    • Find the cause: ambiguous instructions, a flaky tool, randomness in the model.
    • Set the launch bar by the cost of failure, and add fallbacks or human handoff for risky paths.

  6. How would you know if your agent is gaming your eval?

    • Read transcripts, not just scores. Look for shortcuts like hard-coding outputs or editing the tests.
    • Watch for scores that jump without a matching change in real-world metrics.
    • Keep a holdout set the team never tunes against.
    • Grade real outcomes, and check that graders can’t be fooled by output that only looks right.

  7. A new model is 5% more accurate and 3x more expensive. How do you decide?

    • Translate both into business terms: what 5% fewer errors is worth versus the added cost per task.
    • Check where the gains land. Gains on high-stakes tasks matter more than gains on easy ones.
    • Consider latency and reliability, not just average accuracy.
    • Look for a middle path: route only hard cases to the expensive model.

  8. What’s your launch bar for an agent that writes to production systems?

    • Hard guardrails first: permissions, confirmation for irreversible actions, rate limits.
    • High consistency (pass^k) on the risky tasks, not just high average accuracy.
    • Red-team testing for prompt injection and misuse.
    • Staged rollout with monitoring, an audit trail and a kill switch.

  9. How do you evaluate an agent whose correct answer can come in many forms?

    • Grade the outcome, not exact wording or a fixed sequence of steps.
    • Use a rubric of required elements, scored by an LLM judge that’s been checked against humans.
    • Compare against reference answers for meaning, not word-for-word.
    • Accept different valid paths, but still enforce hard rules on the path.

  10. Tell me about an eval you designed. What did it catch, and what did it miss?

    • Situation: the product, and what was at stake if quality slipped.
    • Design: what you graded, how you built the dataset, and which graders you used.
    • Validation: how you showed the grader agreed with human judgment.
    • Impact: a failure it caught before launch, and the business result.
    • Honest gap: what it missed and how you changed the eval afterward.
